Terrain-robust locomotion for a snake robot

Co-adapting a reinforcement-learning controller and passive scale design on real hardware.

View full thesis (PDF · 4.6 MB) ↗
Institution
Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam
Format
Individual research
Thesis grade

Focus

01

The problem

Snake robots can reach places that are difficult for wheeled or legged machines, but a gait that works on one surface may fail on another. I investigated whether the robot could become more robust by adapting both how it moves and how its body contacts the ground.

Does spatial heterogeneity in passive scale design improve terrain-robust locomotion?

The comparison covered homogeneous configurations, where every scale used the same geometry, and heterogeneous configurations that alternated between two designs.

02

My scope

The base robot and parts of its control stack existed before the thesis. My work started from that platform rather than claiming the robot as a ground-up build.

  • Modelled, produced, and tested interchangeable passive scale configurations.
  • Designed and ran the carpet and cardboard experiments on physical hardware.
  • Trained and evaluated Soft Actor-Critic locomotion policies for each design.
  • Built a critic-guided particle swarm optimisation step to refine morphology.
  • Analysed both successful and failed refinement runs.

03

Method

  1. 01

    Change the contact geometry

    Scale width and attack angle defined the physical search space. Homogeneous designs shared one pair of parameters; heterogeneous designs alternated two pairs.

  2. 02

    Learn the gait

    A Soft Actor-Critic agent learned locomotion for each scale configuration, with repeated trials on carpet and cardboard.

  3. 03

    Refine the body

    The learned critic estimated candidate morphologies. Particle swarm optimisation then proposed a design intended to improve the weaker terrain.

  4. 04

    Return to hardware

    Proposed configurations were manufactured and tested on the real robot rather than accepted from simulation or critic estimates alone.

04

Results

The strongest result came from the lower-dimensional homogeneous search. Optimisation raised its worst-terrain displacement from 28.2 cm to 54.4 cm. The best unrefined heterogeneous design was already robust at 50.4 cm, but its subsequent refinement did not transfer to the robot.

05

What failed

The heterogeneous optimiser proposed a configuration whose worst-terrain result dropped from 50.4 cm to 6.6 cm. This was not hidden or averaged away.

The likely issue was coverage. A heterogeneous body created a four-dimensional morphology space, while the available physical trials covered only a small part of it. The critic could guide refinement in the simpler homogeneous space, but its estimates were not reliable enough in the larger search.

Interpretation

Passive heterogeneity can produce strong locomotion, but this study did not show that it is always necessary or easier to optimise.

06

Real hardware

The experimental work was as much about making the system dependable as it was about training policies. Motors overheated, one had to be replaced, TPU scales needed repeated printing, and small physical changes could alter a run. Parts of the control code were also undocumented.

Working through those failures changed how I evaluate an AI result: a promising metric is only useful if the full system can reproduce it outside the optimiser.

07

Takeaway

Co-adaptation worked when the morphology search was constrained and sufficiently represented by real trials. The project also showed where it stopped working: a higher-dimensional design space needs broader data or a more cautious refinement method.
